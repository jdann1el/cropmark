# Thumbnail structure study — radio restoration and railway suspense

Date observed: 2026-10-19. Research only; no trial, skill, brief, score, or generated design was changed.

## Evidence boundary

The strongest supported finding is that **a larger title alone does not establish a stronger relationship between the title and the pictured event**. The exact radio examples below give words and hardware comparable visual weight and a deliberate shared boundary. The railway evidence separates the event, viewpoint, silhouette and title. This is a compositional hypothesis supported by inspection, not measured CTR evidence.

The requested set of 2–3 close **professional designer portfolio** examples per theme was **not fully obtained**. Three exact radio thumbnails were inspected on their creator's first-party YouTube feed, but an outside thumbnail designer is not credited. For railway suspense, one credited railway-horror title artwork, one exact platform concept-art project, and one adjacent horror designer portfolio were inspected. The concept-art project is a university final project, not a professional YouTube benchmark. These distinctions matter: the report does not relabel adjacent or student work as proven professional thumbnail practice.

All selected artwork was actually viewed using native CUA in a separate background browser tab. Search snippets identified candidates; they did not supply the visual diagnosis. Estimates below concern the **artwork's own rectangle**, excluding webpage chrome and presentation matting. They are visual estimates, normally ±3–5 percentage points, not pixel measurements or recommended targets. W = image width; H = image height. Feed judgments are inspection judgments, not audience tests. No external image was downloaded.

**Observation:** these are not minimal-word thumbnails: one has seven main words. They preserve prominence by using a large compact block, a legible object crop and bounded supporting metadata. **Inference:** their useful transferable principle is the deliberate division of pictorial and verbal mass, not a word-count limit. All three share one channel system, so they are three artwork examples, not three independent designers or three proofs of an optimum layout.

**Do not universalize:** a left text column; a 24% bottom band; condensed cream/yellow type; a 50/50 division; red or green payoffs; labels showing model numbers. Here the bottom band provides serial identity but consumes almost a quarter of the card. A new channel or a one-off emotional story may gain little from paying that cost. None establishes that every restoration needs before/after, arrows, faces or artificial damage.

## 2. Railway suspense: one direct artwork and two explicitly limited transfers

### 2.1 Craig Groshek — credited “Ghost Train” artwork

[Publisher's original story page and credit](https://www.creepypastastories.com/ghost-train/). The visible page explicitly credits thumbnail art to Craig Groshek. Native capture: `thumbnail-references/ghost-train-credited-artwork.png` under the evidence directory above. This is published railway-horror story artwork, **not demonstrated YouTube performance**. Its visible ratio is approximately 2.3:1, wider than 16:9.

**Observed:** locomotive nose at right, long train receding diagonally toward the middle-left, rails entering from the lower right, storm sky above. The main white title is a broad middle-left strip (~57% W × 22% H); the logo occupies the upper-left (~43% W × 27% H); an author strip is directly below the title (~40% W × 16% H). Locomotive nose is roughly the rightmost 30% W and around two-thirds H. The title runs toward the receding cars; it does not occupy a remote empty corner. Its supporting strip repeats the title's broad slant and follows the train/track movement. Text and scene form a connected left-to-right reading route.

**Viewer read / inference:** the large front of the train presents an approaching presence, while the title names the supernatural premise. At a hypothetical 320px-wide reduction, the main title would remain a substantial visual mass; the author strip and logo would compete with it. No feed reduction was performed for this source, so this is a projection, not a tested result.

**Do not universalize:** damaged lettering, lightning, slanted words, three separate text blocks, or a train covering a fixed percentage. This treatment spends much space on publisher/author identity. Its multi-block hierarchy and wide ratio cannot be copied directly into a 16:9 night-platform brief.

### 2.2 Alexander Nash — *Villager 301* platform value studies

[Author's complete process page](https://alexandernashportfolio.format.com/dm3103-negotiated-final-project-villager-301), specifically “High fidelity - train station thumbnails” and the completed train-station image. Captures: `thumbnail-references/villager301-platform-value-studies.png` and `thumbnail-references/villager301-platform-final-observed-crop.png`.

**Scope:** a university concept/narrative-design project; “thumbnail” here means a compositional sketch, not YouTube packaging. This is useful as an exact **platform scene construction** reference, not a professional finish standard or type-layout precedent. Final color capture shows a viewport crop; do not infer the full image's exact bounds from it.

**Author explanation (paraphrase):** the last two sketches use a shoulder-level viewpoint to imply someone watching the player from behind. The author selected the train sketch, then used red train lights against a blue environment to make approaching danger the focal point. This is the author's intention, not audience research.

**Observed in the full grayscale sketch sheet:** the selected right-hand image has a near character low in the frame (~20% W × 35% H), a large train face at right (~30% W × 50% H), and converging platform/roof edges between them. A large dark pillar/roof frame occupies the left and top. No headline is present. The figure's position and the train's facing direction explain a relationship: **a viewer/protagonist is looking toward an arrival**, with the station framing both. In the final visible color crop, train lights become the brightest chromatic event, while a red rim on the near figure connects it to that event. Detail remains subdued.

**Inference:** the train/protagonist relationship supplies an event that a title can intensify. Our reference question should therefore include “what is happening or about to happen?” alongside “where can the words fit?”

**Do not universalize:** red means danger; blue is required for horror; a foreground character is mandatory; larger figures are always better; Dutch angles always create suspense. The student drawing's subtle dark-on-dark linework would also need its own feed-scale verification before being used as packaging.

### 2.3 Papiya Graphics — horror subject/type pairs

[Designer-owned Behance project](https://www.behance.net/gallery/250103317/Horror-Cinematic-YouTube-Thumbnail-Design), the two landscape images immediately after the opening portfolio montage: left, a man biting his nails with a three-line Bangla title; right, a man in a backward cap screaming, one hand to his cheek, with a white/yellow Bangla block. Native capture: `thumbnail-references/papiya-horror-type-object-pairs.png`.

**Scope:** actual horror-thumbnail designer work, but **neither frame is a platform scene**. It supplies a controlled comparison for type/subject massing only. The author describes intent to improve engagement; no CTR results or split-test data were supplied. The script is not translated here; visual structure can be observed without inventing a textual meaning.

**Observed:** both subjects fill the left ~50–58% W and nearly the full H; hair/shoulders are deliberately cropped. Right text blocks occupy ~45–48% W × 44–50% H, around the subject's eye/mouth region rather than tucked against the outer edge. The largest middle/final line projects toward the subject, with a narrow gap and, locally, near overlap. Thus upper-right placement is not itself the failure: the neighboring large face, shared vertical center and compact multiline block make that region part of a paired composition. Background clues and textures are weaker than the subject and words.

**Do not universalize:** add a screaming face; red grading; yellow key words; extreme distress; equal halves. Those would change the promise of restrained platform suspense. This reference helps explain **why some right-side text is anchored**, not why every horror scene should use this style.

## 3. Diagnosis of the frozen H1/H2 images

Read-only visual inspection used `outputs/cropmark-review/deep-study/H1-revealed.png` and `H2-revealed.png`. Their labels are treated as provided. Nothing was regenerated or scored here.

**H1 radio:** the candidate expands the single-line question to roughly 82% W and ~17% H, above a radio occupying roughly 80% W. The text is not small. It now forms a broad header over an already dominant centered object. The baseline's two-line block is narrower (~37% W) and taller (~32% H) and projects directly onto the radio's left side. The composition difference is therefore not “readable versus unreadable.” It is the kind of verbal/pictorial relationship: headline-over-object versus a compact interruption of the object area. The common radio photograph is detailed and identifiable; typography changes alone supply limited additional evidence of rescue, failure or transformation. This does not establish that covering the speaker is preferable; the human preference result remains the relevant evidence for this trial.

**H2 platform:** all three images retain the same broad concept: small lone figure in a lighted recession, bright wet platform path, empty track, upper-right title. The candidate's title is substantial (~34% W × 33% H); it is not a micro-caption. The figure occupies only approximately 3–4% W but its lit silhouette and long shadow create a clear image focal point. Enlarging the words makes two strong, laterally separated masses: figure/light corridor versus title. It does not show what makes this arrival, departure or “last” moment wrong. The suspense already exists; the title's size is not the entire missing cause. Whether an explicit second presence/arrival would help or spoil the premise is an unresolved creative question, not a rule to implement.

**Research direction:** investigate the title/object relationship and the scene's event together, with deliberate alternatives for the gap, crop and connection. Do not implement the three reference layouts as templates. A future review can ask, at the actual target display size: (1) what is recognized first, (2) what precise event or question is understood next, and (3) do the title and scene reinforce that same event? These are proposed inspection questions, not altered evaluation gates.
