# Compare the composition, not the explanation

Use this for original work, substantial redesign and visual critique. A tidy layout can still be an unattractive advertisement or a weak thumbnail. Judge the artifact in its audience's situation: what would make someone want this product, understand this argument or open this video?

## Choose a useful comparator

Inspect two actual finished pieces close to this task: communication goal, format, content density and available imagery. A bakery ad is a closer appetite benchmark than a studio branding board. A product advert and a quiet catalogue can both be good, but do different jobs. The atlas is a starting index; search beyond it when its examples do not match. A moodboard, title, author claim or search thumbnail is not an inspected final artwork. Record missing evidence honestly.

Study mechanisms across references rather than averaging their styles. Keep the original frame separate from mockup borders and browser chrome. Do not compare a polished packaging photograph against a flat post without identifying that difference.

## Trace relationships before setting coordinates

In the project's direction record, retain one compact reference trace, with a source link and the inspected image:

- **Masses:** approximate headline-group, subject and brand ink bounds as fractions of the artwork's width/height. Measure visible logo ink, excluding transparent padding. Distinguish type size, cap height and complete wordmark width; “logo at 3%” is ambiguous.
- **Groups:** identify which words form one phrase and which are separate support. Record their ink gaps, relative to adjacent letter height, and the group's anchor. A face change is not a reason to detach half a sentence. Equal gaps between every block are not required: proximity communicates meaning.
- **Space:** compare the outer margins of the whole group, insets of fitted panels, and useful space reserved for the subject. For rotated panels, inspect spacing in the panel's own axes. Do not mistake a broad colour field for an accidentally oversized label.
- **Image:** identify the recognisable parts that must survive, what is intentionally cropped, the eye path, and whether a subject is grounded, floating or behind type. Record the actual source-to-frame crop, not merely `cover` or `fit`.
- **Brand:** choose a role: quiet signature, equal partner, or primary subject. Place it on a named axis or in a real containing region. For a centred lockup compare visible ink gaps; for editorial alignment share an edge. Neither a footer bar nor centring every logo is a default.

Estimates are not hidden design specifications. Mark their uncertainty and use them to choose relationships for this brief. Do not impose a reference's percentages universally. Declare the intended gaps, anchors and crop decisions before rendering; do not derive permissive maxima from a bad final layout just to obtain PASS.

## Make the image serve its role

**Hero/product recognition:** preserve enough silhouette and identifying detail to understand and desire the product. Check closures, handles, printed identity, food's crust/crumb and the outline carrying recognition. A tightly cropped texture can be convincing, but cannot silently stand in for the only product introduction.

**Detail/texture:** choose a deliberate detail, a scale at which its material reads, and a crop that looks decisive. A series needs a recognisable hero somewhere; three unrelated crops of one photo do not automatically create three campaign ideas.

**Scene/event:** identify what is happening or about to happen. Enlarging a detached title does not strengthen an unexplained scene. Place words and subject in a shared reading route; preserve the clue that makes this particular headline meaningful at feed size.

Inspect the source before committing. If it already cuts off the required subject, no layout trick restores missing pixels. For a fictional generated asset, revise the photography brief or generate a suitable view when authorized. Preserve a real supplied product; change the framing or disclose the limitation rather than inventing its missing identity. Specify camera/view, subject placement, copy space, complete versus close-up framing and physical contact when sourcing or generating imagery.

Shapes and lines must participate: a real label, a comparison, an object support, a crop mask, a meaningful typographic gesture or a supplied brand language. An orange doorway surrounding an everyday bottle is not specific merely because its rationale says “routine.” Do not replace every frame with a circle, every rule with an asterisk, or every body with a fashionable serif.

## Review as viewer, then as art director

Put the representative and its close comparator beside each other at the same target display size. First inspect without reading the rationale:

1. What do I recognise first, and what does the image make me expect? Does that match the actual message?
2. What is the attractive or informative proposition? Is the subject inviting/convincing, or merely present in a clean rectangle?
3. Where is ours visibly weaker: title/subject relationship, material, crop, typographic rhythm, density, brand placement or finish? Name the region and consequence. “Looks professional” and a 4/5 score are not observations.
4. Does the complete phrase read as one thought? Does the logo look placed deliberately? Is any required text/identity clipped? Are near contacts accidental, or do contact/overlap and lighting visibly establish the intended relationship?

Then inspect full size for masks, glyphs and precise spacing. Keep three concrete comparison observations in the record; if a reference outperforms the piece, correct the underlying cause before extending the set. If no fair comparator is inspectable, record that limitation rather than claiming reference-level finish. Use an independent reviewer when available; otherwise perform a separate viewer pass and disclose that it is self-review.

The maximum three correction rounds still applies. At the limit report the unresolved visual gap; a geometrically valid file is not visual acceptance. A deliberately expressive crop, asymmetric grid or tiny functional signature can pass when its observed effect supports the task. A written justification alone cannot make it pass.
